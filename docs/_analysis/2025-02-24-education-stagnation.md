The NY Mag article traces a decade-plus of flat or declining U.S. academic outcomes and argues the pandemic merely exposed a longer slide in the political compact around public schooling.

**Signals from the data**
- NAEP math and reading have trended sideways or down since the early 2010s; pandemic shocks deepened losses and widened gaps.
- Spending rose, but dollars increasingly went to fixed costs (pensions, healthcare) rather than classroom instruction or tutoring.
- Affluent families compensated with pods/tutors during COVID, accelerating divergence from lower-income peers.

**Political realignment around schools**
- Traditional “save public schools” coalition is frayed: GOP pushes vouchers/charters; parts of the left distrust testing/accountability; centrists focus on culture wars.
- Teacher burnout and staffing churn reduce the system’s slack to absorb shocks or reform.

**Interventions with evidence behind them**
- High-dosage tutoring and extended learning time show consistent gains, but scale-up has been uneven and underfunded.
- Targeted cash for ventilation, attendance outreach, and reading coaches beat broad, unfocused spends.
- Transparent reporting by grade/subject (not just overall averages) keeps attention on the steepest losses.

**Takeaway**
The article argues the U.S. lacks a stable political majority for hard, measurable improvement in public schools. Until spending prioritizes proven interventions—and accountability is insulated from culture wars—test scores are likely to remain stagnant and inequities to widen.
